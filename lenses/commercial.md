# Commercial Judgment

Running a partnership well and structuring the deal well are different skills. This lens covers the second: setting a number, holding it under pressure, and telling your own side when a term doesn't work. It returns a position you can defend in the room.

Terms used here: **margin** is what you keep per unit after paying the partner. **Take rate** is the share of each transaction the platform keeps. **RPM** is revenue per thousand impressions, the usual way to compare how well different traffic earns.

## Margin or volume

1. **Decide on margin under realistic demand, not on sticker price.** A price cut only wins if volume grows faster than per-unit margin falls. Say why you expect that out loud. "Let's just get the volume" is not a reason.
2. **Thin margin is an exception someone signs for.** Set a floor in advance and require a named approver below it. Without the floor, every rep's pressure call becomes the new default.
3. **If you give a lot, say what it buys.** A new vertical, a logo that opens other doors, or something you need to learn are real reasons. Pretending a heavy discount is a normal deal hides the cost.
4. **A rate you could charge isn't always one you should.** Charging the maximum leaves room for a cheaper competitor to undercut you on the same supply, or for both sides to go direct once you've introduced them. Ask what actually stops that before you lock the number.
5. **Kill thin-margin leads early.** If the unit economics are structurally weak and there's no volume path, say so on day one. Ideas get more serious just because nobody stopped them.
6. **Split a blended rate before judging it.** One average across all traffic hides which source is dragging it down. Compare the number with and without the suspect slice.
7. **Set a spend minimum and a per-unit minimum together.** A spend floor alone lets a low-priced buyer clear the bar while losing you money on every unit.

## Setting the split

1. **Anchor first, with a number you can defend.** Final numbers tend to land near the first credible anchor, not the midpoint.
2. **Base the split on who contributes what.** Whose traffic, whose integration cost, whose brand risk. Agree on that first and the percentage follows. With several intermediaries, run the stacked math yourself; a fee chain that sounds fair can leave you almost nothing.
3. **Every concession comes with an ask.** "I can move on the rate if we lock a 12-month minimum." A free concession invites the next request. Sometimes changing the structure (a flat fee instead of a percentage) solves their objection without moving your economics.
4. **Write guarantees as floors with a clear exit.** The partner gets a specific trigger they can act on if you miss. You avoid an open-ended promise you can't keep.
5. **Know your walk-away before the call.** If you can't really walk, lean on the constraints the other side has, like their timeline or switching cost. Bluffing a walk-away usually shows.
6. **Renegotiate when the terms stop working, not when the relationship feels shaky.** Quietly honoring a deal that no longer clears your bar is the more common failure than a tough counterpart.
7. **Say who carries currency risk.** A fixed payout in one currency to a partner paid in another leaks margin slowly. Quote in their currency or make the rate adjustable.

## Pricing both sides of a platform

1. **Price each side by how easily it leaves.** The side with more options, usually supply, often needs gentler terms, even if that looks lopsided.
2. **Assume partners will compare rates.** A pricing plan that only works if nobody learns what a peer pays won't last.
3. **Tier by size when partner volume varies a lot.** One flat rate overcharges your smallest partners and undercharges the ones big enough to walk.

## Risk and escalation

1. **Check whether a complaint is factual before conceding.** Pull the data that settles it. A measurable problem and a perception problem need different answers.
2. **Price the opportunity cost.** A deal above your floor can still be wrong if it ties up capacity a better partner is waiting for.
3. **Give concentration risk a number and a fix.** "This partner would be X% of revenue" belongs next to a notice clause or diversification target.
4. **Write your escalation triggers down before the pressure arrives,** with a named decider for each. Escalate on those triggers and hold the line yourself on everything else.

## Output

- The decision being made and the number at stake.
- Recommended position, with the floor and walk-away stated.
- Each concession on the table paired with what you ask for in return.
- Risks that need sign-off, and who signs.
